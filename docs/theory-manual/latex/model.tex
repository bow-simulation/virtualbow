\chapter{Bow Model}

A scientific model is a simplified and abstracted representation of reality, often expressed in a mathematical way.
A good model reduces the complexity of a real system by capturing only its most significant aspects and neglecting less significant ones.
Analyzing this simplified representation can then lead to conclusions about the real system that wouldn't have been possible otherwise.

Which aspects of reality a model must reflect depends on the kinds of questions it is intended to answer.
The first step in developing a bow model is therefore to clarify its scope and intended application.

\section{Scope of the Model}
\label{section:model-scope}

In the case of VirtualBow, the purpose of the bow model is to give users the ability to evaluate the bow designs they come up with.
So what characteristics make a bow design a good one and what are the implications for the bow model?
Many things could be considered, but we mainly focus on the following three categories:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Viability}: Can the design be realized in practice?
The most important limitation on a bow's viability is whether the chosen materials can withstand the loads that occur during use.
To assess this, the model must accurately predict the distribution of stress across the limbs as well as the tensile forces in the string throughout the shot cycle.
A less common, but still very important limitation to the viability of a design is stability.
As an example, imagine a bow with deep recurves and narrow limbs.
If taken too far, such a design might be prone to the limbs twisting and the bow "flipping around".
Other designs might "flip flop" between two states where the tension switches between the lower and upper limb.
Both might happen long before any material limit has been reached.
The common reason is that one of the equilibrium states of the bow has been unstable, meaning that the system has no tendency to return to this state after a small disturbance.
This is something that VirtualBow cannot yet predict, but falls well within this category and is therefore planned for the future.

\item \textbf{Performance}: Being viable is of course only the bottom line for a bow.
Ideally we also want to get good performance out of it.
Important performance characteristics include the arrow's velocity and the bow's degree of efficiency, i.e. the percentage of the stored elastic energy that is transferred to the arrow during the shot.
To capture these, the model has to represent all major mechanisms through which a bow loses efficiency.
We will examine these mechanisms in more detail when reviewing existing research.

\item \textbf{Comfort}: Raw performance is not everything either.
A bow design is also judged by how it feels to the archer: the draw force, the shape of the draw curve (does it feel smooth or is there stacking) and the dynamic forces exerted to the shooter (hand shock).
Acoustic vibrations technically belong in this category as well, but this is where we draw the line to keep the complexity manageable.
\end{enumerate}

Any physical effects in archery that don't significantly affect one of those categories above fall outside the scope of VirtualBow.
One example is \textit{exterior ballistics}, i.e. everything that happens to the arrow and its flight path after it leaves the bow.
Another example is the elastic flexing motion of the arrow depending on its dynamic spine and its interaction with the string and the arrow rest.
Both topics are important in their own right, but they do not significantly feed back into the fundamental design decisions of a bow and are therefore not included in the model.

Another decision that VirtualBow has to make is what kinds of bow designs it supports.
There is a wide variety of design elements in bow making and it would be impractical to support each and every one of them.
Not only from the perspective of physical modelling but also from a UI perspective, keeping the user interface simple for the most common types of bows while still ideally making more complex designs possible.
That said, some of the limitations that VirtualBow currently places on bow designs are just technical limitations, not some deliberate decisions, and will hopefully be lifted in the future.
An example is that cross sections have to be rectangular and made up of horizontal layers.
This is just a result of VirtualBow first focussing on the most common case of \mbox{(glass-)laminated} bows, since that's how most modern bows are being made.
In the long term, non-rectangular cross sections and more complex internal compositions are very desirable features.
Another major design limitation imposed by VirtualBow is that of symmetry: The upper and lower limbs are identical to each other and the pivot point of the handle and the nocking point on the string are both located exactly at the center of the bow.
In real bows, however, due to the arrow rest being located above the handle's pivot point, almost every bow is slightly asymmetric, even if we disregard extreme cases such as the typical Japanese Yumi longbow.
This asymmetry is usually compensated by one limb being slighty stronger than the other, giving the bow either a positive, neutral or negative tiller depending on the desired shooting style.
Whether asymmetry will ever be included in VirtualBow is not entirely clear yet.
On one hand it is a property that almost every bow has, on the other hand it is for most bows more a question of fine-tuning rather than something that has a big impact on the fundamental properties of a bow design as defined by the three categories above.

Having clarified the scope of VirtualBow's internal bow model at least somewhat, the next question becomes: What does a bow model have to look like in order to capture the things listed above?
That's not easy to answer and usually there is a certain amount of experience as well as trial and error involved in physical modelling before getting it right.
Luckily there has already been done some scientific research about bow and arrow physics in the past that we can draw inspiration from.
The next section therefore examines some of that research before we go ahead and define our own model in detail.

\section{Literature Review}

Research about the mechanics of bow and arrow goes back to at least the 1930s, when C.N.~Hickman, P.E.~Klopstek and others performed systematic experiments and developed simple mathematical models.
Without modern computers available, these early models were still limited in their complexity by what could be solved analytically.
Their focus was therefore on qualitative insight rather than accurate prediction.
One such early models is the~\textit{Hickman Model}~\cite{bib:hi37}, published in~1937.
It assumes the bow to consist of two rigid limbs that are connected to the stationary midsection via elastic hinges as shown in figure~\ref{fig:model:hickman}.
Even though this is obviously a huge simplification, this model can already be used to explain the general shape of the force-draw curve of a bow (including stacking) and to investigate the bow's efficiency, i.e. how it transfers its stored elastic energy to an arrow.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{figures/model/hickman}
\caption{Hickman model as used by W.C. Marlow \cite{bib:ma80}}
\label{fig:model:hickman}
\end{figure}

An important modification to the Hickman model was suggested by W.C.~Marlow in 1980~\cite{bib:ma80}.
It was already known that the original Hickman model severely overpredicted a bow's degree of efficiency.
Marlow mentions predicted efficiencies over 90\% while measurements suggest values between 70\% and 85\% for most bows.
Popular explanations for this discrepancy were effects like hysteresis and air resistance.
Marlow however found another reason: With the assumption of an inextensible string, as was the case in Hickman's original model, the arrow exits the bow exactly at brace height, where the limbs are exactly at the reversal point of their motion and therefore have zero velocity.
Only the kinetic energy of the string remains in the bow, the rest is transferred to the arrow.
Marlow then showed that accounting for the elasticity of the string shifts the point of exit for the arrow away from the braced position, such that the limbs still have substantial kinetic energy left.
In his numerical example this modification reduces the predicted efficiency to a much more realistic~78\% as opposed to~92\% previously.
Of the energy remaining in the bow,~11\% were the kinetic energy of the limbs,~9\% the kinetic energy of the string and~2\% the potential energy of limbs and string.

An actual elastic limb, as opposed to the rigid simplification used in Hickman's model, might not have such a clear reversal point with zero kinetic energy.
But there is still a relevant lesson in Marlow's work: The efficiency of a bow is mainly determined by the kinetic energy that stays in the system due to mass and elasticity of the string and the mass distribution of the limbs. The contribution of dissipative effects like air resistance and hysteresis to the overall efficiency was estimated by Marlow to be less than~2\% and~3\% respectively.
This is good news for us, because those dissipative effects are very tricky to model accurately.
But we will still have to think about them eventually, because a model without any form of motion decay won't look very convinving either.

Moving  forward in time, the widespread availability of digital computers eventually made it possible to simulate far more detailed models of bows and arrows, enabling much more accurate predictions.
Significant contributions in this area were made by Dr.~Bob~Kooi and his co-authors, who throughout the~1980s and~1990s published numerous papers on bow and arrow mechanics, applying numerical methods to solve increasingly complex mathematical models.

The first one of those papers is titled \textit{On the static deformation of a bow}~\cite{bib:kosp80} and investigates the storage of deformation energy in bows with and without recurve.
Figure~\ref{fig:model:kooi} was taken from that paper and gives an impression of the model used there.
The bow is assumed to be symmetric and has an inextensible string.
The limb is represented as an Euler-Bernoulli beam with varying bending stiffness along its length.
We will have a closer look at the variuos beam theories, including Euler-Bernoulli, later.
Kooi uses the general form of the Euler-Bernoulli beam's differential equation that accounts for arbitrarily large deformations.
Combining this with geometric constraints for the string and some boundary conditions leads to the bow's equations of equilibrium, a free-boundary value problem for a set of ordinary differential equations. The equations of equilibrium are solved numerically for specific draw lengths of the bow.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/model/kooi}
\caption{Bow model used by Bob Kooi \cite{bib:kosp80}}
\label{fig:model:kooi}
\end{figure}

In subsequent papers titled \textit{On the mechanics of the bow and arrow}~\cite{bib:kooi81} and \textit{On the mechanics of the modern working-recurve bow}~\cite{bib:kooi91} Kooi also considers the dynamic case.
He uses a linear-elastic string, like Marlow, and whose mass is distributed to two point masses at the string center and the limb tip.
The string itself does not have any dynamic behaviour on its own, it especially doesn't bend or flex in the transversal direction.
Instead it just acts as an ideal spring between the string center and either the limb tip or the contact point of the string on the bow limb.
The arrow is modeled as a concentrated point mass sitting at the center of the string.
The equations of motion for the system now form a \textit{moving-boundary value problem for a set of partial differential equations}.
They are solved numerically by using a \textit{finite-difference method with complete discretization}.
This means that both space and time are approximated by a discrete "grid" of values.
The partial derivatives in the governing equations are then approximated by finite difference quotients operating on those grid points.
This results in a large, but finite, number of difference equations for the unknowns that can be solved with a computer program.

In the paper \textit{On the Mechanics of the Arrow: Archer's Paradox}~\cite{bib:kosp97}, Kooi refines his model even more by including an elastic arrow with a sliding contact to the bow's grip. This makes it possible to analyze the Archer's Paradox: The phenomenon of arrows going around the grip of a bow by performing a bending motion in the horizontal plane.
The dynamics of the arrow are however treated partly decoupled from the bow's dynamics: Only the influence of the bow on the arrow's motion is considered, the other way around is neglected.
This is in line with our assumption from section~\ref{section:model-scope} that the bending motion of the arrow doesn't contribute much to a bow's overall performance and is therefore out of scope for our model.

\section{Beam Theories}

At this point it makes sense to take a brief look at different beam theories.
A beam, in this context, is a slender structural element whose length is much greater than its cross‑sectional dimensions, and which can resist loads applied perpendicular to its longitudinal axis through bending, shear, and sometimes torsion.
All beam theories share the same basic approach: they reduce the mechanical properties of each cross‑section to an idealized, infinitely thin centerline, which then serves as the basis for analyzing the beam's static or dynamic behaviour.

\subsection*{Linear vs Nonlinear}

In many engineering applications, a beam can be assumed to bend only slightly.
These small deflections do not change the shape of the beam enough to significantly affect its internal balance of forces.
If the materials are also linear-elastic - meaning stresses increase proportionally with strains - then the entire beam responds in a linear way: increasing the externally applied loads proportionally increases the deformation.
Problems in this linear regime can usually be solved analytically, with many solutions to standard cases available in textbooks.
According to the preconditions listed above, linear beam theory can break down for two main causes of nonlinear behavior:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Geometric nonlinearity}: The assumption of small deformations does not hold. The beam deflects enough such that the balance of forces in the final, deflected state is no longer similar to that in the original, undeflected state.
\item \textbf{Material nonlinearity}: The material does not follow a simple proportional relationship between stress and strain.
\end{enumerate}

Most common materials - including those used for making bows - behave linearly up to a certain level of strain, called the proportional limit (see figure \ref{fig:model:proportional-limit}).
After this limit, the stress-strain curve begins to deviate from the linear projection.
Plastic (irreversible) deformation and eventually failure of the material follow.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{figures/model/proportional-limit}
\caption{Linear vs. nonlinear material behaviour}
\label{fig:model:proportional-limit}
\end{figure}

If we assume the materials in a bow to operate safely below their elastic limit, which is the point where plastic deformation sets in, they will usually also stay within their proportional limit.
Assuming linear material behavior in a bow therefore seems reasonable, we just have to keep in mind that the simulation results become invalid if stresses beyond the proportional limit occur.
But at that point the bow design usually has to be rejected anyway.
The deflections of a bow limb, however, are far too large as to treat them in a geometrically linear way, making the problem geometrically nonlinear even if the material itself remains linear-elastic.
Geometrcally nonlinear bending problems can't generally be solved analytically and therefore require numerical methods for computing an approximate solution.

\subsection*{Cross-section Kinematics}

Having established that we need the nonlinear form of any beam theory we might consider, the main point of distinction between the various beam theories becomes how they assume the cross-sections to deform in relation to the deformation of the beam's centerline.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/model/beam-theories}
\caption{Deformation of a beam segment according to different beam theories (exaggerated)}
\label{fig:model:beam-theories}
\end{figure}

The oldest and most widely used beam theory is the Euler-Bernoulli theory that we already came across in Kooi's bow models.
In this theory, all cross-sections of a beam are subject to the following assumptions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Initially planar cross-sections stay planar during the deformation of the beam
\item Cross-sections perpendicular to beam's centerline stay perpendicular to that line while it deforms
\end{enumerate}

In figure \ref{fig:model:beam-theories}, which schematically shows a deformed segment of a beam with its centerline, the Euler-Bernoulli case is shown as the blue, planar section perpendicular to the beam's centerline.
A more accurate but also more complex beam theory is the Timoshenko theory.
It relaxes the assumption of perpendicular cross-sections.
This is shown in figure \ref{fig:model:beam-theories} as the purple section, allowed to rotate by the so-called shear angle $\gamma$ with respect to the perpendicular orientation.
But even this theory is not perfect, because cross-sections of a deforming beam don't stay exactly planar: they actually warp in a complicated way, which more advanced beam theories attempt to capture in varying amount of detail.

So how do those theories affect the overall behaviour of a beam and in which situations does the improved accuracy of more advanced theories make a difference?
Consider a simple cantilever beam as shown in figure \ref{fig:model:section-forces}, clamped on the left side and subjected to a transversal tip force $F$ at the right.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/model/section-forces}
\caption{Cross-section forces of a cantilever beam with tip load}
\label{fig:model:section-forces}
\end{figure}

Let's call the length along the beam axis $s$.
At each length $s$ there are three internal forces that can potentially act on the cross-section: a normal force $N$ perpendicular to the section, a shear force $Q$ in parallel to the section and a bending moment $M$ around the section's rotation axis.
In the static case, those are determined by the condition that each section has to be in balance with the externally applied load $F$.
For this cantilever beam this means that the normal force $N$ is zero over the length $s$, the shear force $Q$ is constant and opposed to $F$ and the bending moment $M$ has a linear distribution, since it is proportional to the "lever arm" between the section in question and the external force.
Each of the section forces has a different effect with regards to the deformation of the beam:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The bending moment $M$}, as already given away by its name, causes a bending deformation of the beam as shown in figure \ref{fig:bending-vs-shear} (1).
The cross sections develop a linear stress and strain distribution in the normal direction.
This bending stress is greatest at the top and bottom surface of the beam and zero at a single point within, defining the so-called neutral fiber.
The resultant of those bending stresses is the bending moment $M$.
\item \textbf{The shear force $Q$} also causes a distinct contribution to the beam's deformation, as shown in figure \ref{fig:bending-vs-shear} (2).
The shear deformation corresponds to stresses and strains in parallel to the section and distorts the beam into a parallelogram-like shape.
Note how the shear deformation causes the beam's cross-sections to rotate relative to the centerline.
\item \textbf{The normal force $N$}, if present, would extend or contract the beam in the longitudinal direction.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/model/bending-vs-shear}
\caption{Deflection of a beam as a sum of bending, shear and warping}
\label{fig:bending-vs-shear}
\end{figure}

As figure \ref{fig:bending-vs-shear} furthermore shows, the \textbf{total deformation (3)} of the beam is a combination of the \textbf{bending deformation (1)} and the \textbf{shear deformation (2}) and also includes the warping of the cross-sections.

The Euler-Bernoulli theory only captures the bending aspect of the deformation.
This is because shear deformation, as we saw, requires the cross-sections to rotate relative to the beam's centerline, which the Euler-Bernoulli theory does not permit.
The Timoshenko theory, on the other hand, does capture the additional shear deformation for exactly this reason.
So how relevant is this limitation of the Euler-Bernoulli theory?
Does shear deformation actually meaningfully contribute to the overall deflection of a beam?
This depends in large part on the "slenderness" of the beam.
The more slender a beam is, i.e. the higher the slenderness ratio $l/h$ of length to cross section height, the more dominant the bending deformation becomes over the shear deformation and the more accurate the Euler-Bernoulli theory becomes.
Short, stubby beams on the other hand can show significant shear deformation, requiring more advanced theories like Timoshenko's.
This becomes plausible if we look at figure \ref{fig:model:section-forces} again: The shear force $Q$ is constant, it only depends on the external force $F$ and does not increase when the beam is made longer.
The peak of the bending moment $M$ however increases with the length of the beam, due to the increasing leverage, leading to the bending effect becoming more dominant relative to the shearing.

\subsection*{Selection of a beam theory}

Ideally we would like to choose the most simple theory that is accurate enough for our use case.
Therefore the question we need to answer is whether or not a bow limb is slender enough to justify the application of the Euler-Bernoulli theory.
A common rule of thumb states that beams with a slenderness ratio of $l/h > 10$ are a good match.
It only takes a few back-of-the-envelope calculations to realize that basically all reasonable bow limb designs far exceed this minimum slenderness requirement, which seems to justify the use of the Euler-Bernoulli theory.
But there is one more complication that we have to consider.
Shear deformation cannot only become important in short and stubby beams, but also in composite beams where layers of very soft and very stiff materials are combined with each other.

As an example, let's look at a classical sandwich beam as shown in figure \ref{fig:sandwich-beam}.
It consists of a soft and light core of thickness $t_c$ and shear modulus $G_c$, covered by two comparably much more stiff face sheets of thickness $t_f$ and with a Young's modulus of $E_f$.
The figure shows how the beam would deform under pure bending (green/dashed line) and under the additional influence of the core's shear deformation (red/solid line).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/model/sandwich-beam}
\caption{Deformation of a sandwich beam}
\label{fig:sandwich-beam}
\end{figure}

According to \cite{bib:zenkert1995}, the deflection of such a beam, assuming that the stiffness of the face sheets is much higher than that of the core, is calculated as the sum of bending deflection $w_b$ and shear deflection $w_s$ as

\begin{equation}
w = w_b + w_s, \quad w_s = \frac{FL}{S}, \quad w_b = \frac{FL^3}{3D}
\end{equation}

with the shear stiffness $S = \frac{G_c d^2}{t_c}$ and the bending stiffness $D = \frac{E_f t_f d^2}{2}$ and $d = t_c + t_f$ being the distance between the center of the faces.
This assumes small deflections, i.e. linear beam theory, but that's okay for this simple consideration.
Since we are interested in the relevance of the shear deflection compared to the total deflection, we calculate the ratio of the two as

\begin{equation}
\eta = \frac{w_s}{w} = \frac{w_s}{w_b + w_s} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{SL^2}{3D}}
\end{equation}

The combination of a wooden core with fiberglass sheets, which is probably the most common pairing for modern bows, produces negligible shear deflection of just $0.02\%$ in this example.
Switching the sheets from glass to carbon, which has a higher tensile stiffness, increases the shear deflection to $0.08\%$, which is still fairly small.
Relevant shear deflection only starts to happen in this example with more exotic core materials, like synthetic foams.
The material properties of foams that are actually used in modern bow limbs are not known to the author, so two hypothetical foams, stiff and soft, have been selected to cover a plausible range.
Although it is probably safe to say that at least the soft foam, leading to a significant shear deflection of $10.71\%$, is not strong enough to actually work as a bow material in this combination.

This rough calculation suggests that shear deformation in a bow is negligible in most practical situations, which makes Euler–Bernoulli beam theory a good general modeling choice.
Nevertheless, extreme material combinations where shear effects become significant might be possible, so it’s worth keeping this in mind as a potential limitation of the model and a future point of improvement.

\section{Model Definition}

We have now learned all we need to know in order to define our own bow model.
First of all, a few words on the general modeling approach.
As we saw in the previous section, any decently complex bow model cannot be solved analytically but has to be combined with some sort of numerical method for calculating an approximate solution.
Models based on continuum mechanics, such as the Euler-Bernoulli beam theory, possess infinitely many degrees of freedom.
This means that their state cannot be represented by a finite number of variables, only by continuous functions such as the bending line in case of the Euler-Bernoulli theory.
In order to be solvable by a computer program, such a model will eventually have to be approximated by a discrete model with finitely many degrees of freedom.
This step is called discretization.
Kooi for example, like a true mathematician, formulates the complete equations of motion for his continuous bow model and only then starts to employ a discretization technique, in his case a finite difference method, to compute a solution for those equations.
This results in a clear separation between the mathematical model and the numerical solution method.

In contrast, we're going to use a more engineering-like approach instead by starting with a discrete system in the first place.
We do this by constructing the continuous parts of the bow from a large number of so-called finite elements.
A finite element can be interpreted as an approximation of a little part of a continuous structure, but with only finitely many degrees of freedom.
Those finite degrees of freedom are the positions and rotation angles of the nodes at which the elements are connected to each other.
The more elements are being used, the better the approximation gets and the closer the results will be to the exact solution.
The finite element method leads to a system of ordinary differential equations, one for each degree of freedom, which can be solved by standard numerical methods as we will see later.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/model/bow-simulator}
\caption{Finite element model of the bow}
\label{fig:model:bow-simulator}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:model:bow-simulator} shows the finite element model of our bow.
It is symmetric with respect to the horizontal plane and assumes the limbs to satisfy the Euler-Bernoulli assumptions, constructing them from geometrically nonlinear Euler-Bernoulli beam elements.
A concentrated point mass at the limb tip accounts for any additional weight that might be placed there, like tip overlays for example.

The string model already has some history behind it.
Up to VirtualBow version 0.9, the string has been modeled as a chain of point masses and linear springs.
This model had a higher fidelity than that of Kooi's, since it also captured the transversal dynamics of the string.
The differences were especially apparent in the motion of the string after the arrow left the bow and for very light arrows.
But this model also caused a number of issues.
The chain-like setup sometimes behaved in strange and unexpected ways and tended to cause numerical problems for the simulation.
The additional degrees of freedom made the model more complicated without actually improving the accuracy of key simulation results.
Therefore, starting with version 0.10, a string model much more like Kooi's has been adopted.
As shown in figure~\ref{fig:model:bow-simulator}, the string now consists of a single spring-like element, with some special treatment for possible contact with the limb for recurve bows, and whose mass is distributed to two point masses placed at the string center and the string tip.
We limit ourselves to linear-elastic string materials for pragmatic reasons, since obtaining the exact nonlinear stress-strain curve of a particular string material is pretty difficult.
Instead, any possible material nonlinearity will have to be accounted for by using an estimated average stiffness.

The arrow is another simple point mass, without any additional flexing motion, as already justified earlier.
It is connected to the string by an ideal, frictionless contact that releases the arrow cleanly as soon as the contact force becomes negative and exceeds a specified clamping force.

As we have seen, modeling dissipative effects is not extremely important for simulating the performance of a bow.
However, a simulation without any energy dissipation doesn't really "look right", especially after the string has left the bow.
So VirtualBow uses a crude way to include some configurable damping on the system level by assigning a desired damping ratio to the limbs and to the string, respectively.
How this works in detail will be explained later.

All in all, there are four different types of elements present in this model:

\begin{itemize}
\item Beam Elements (Limbs)
\item String Element (String)
\item Mass Elements (Arrow + additional masses)
\end{itemize}

All of them have to account for arbitrarily large movements and are therefore geometrically nonlinear.

In the next chapters we will first show how the equations of motion for a finite element system are assembled from the equations of motion for the single elements.
We will then derive the equations of motion for the element types listed above.
Finally we're going to discuss the numerical solution of those equations for the static and dynamic case and how this is implemented in VirtualBow.
